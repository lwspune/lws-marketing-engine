# PYQVault InstaRead --- Science, Assembled

## Concept universe and storyboard bible for cinematic "mechanical build-up" educational Reels

**Version:** 1.0\
**Purpose:** A production brief that can be handed to Claude Code / a
video-generation workflow to build repeatable, high-quality vertical
science animations for PYQVault InstaRead.\
**Visual premise:** Take the satisfying *assembly language* of a
futuristic suit-up sequence---parts fly in, align, lock, connect,
activate---and use it to make an actual Physics or Chemistry principle
visible. Create an original visual identity; do not reproduce Iron
Man/Marvel suit designs, logos, signature shots, or footage.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 1. Creative north star

## 1.1 The format

Every Reel should feel like a small cinematic reveal:

1.  **Mystery / hook:** An object, structure, or system floats in a dark
    space.
2.  **Assembly:** Components enter in a deliberate sequence. Each
    component corresponds to a meaningful part of the concept.
3.  **Activation:** Current flows, a field appears, a reaction proceeds,
    a wave propagates, or a mechanism moves.
4.  **Explanation:** The visual reveals the underlying principle---not
    merely a decorative effect.
5.  **Exam payoff:** One formula, rule, or misconception is resolved in
    a memorable final frame.

The central creative rule is: **the build-up must teach.** If a part
appears, the viewer should learn what it does. If a glow appears, it
should represent something real (energy transfer, current, field
visualization, reaction progress) rather than generic "science magic."

## 1.2 Recommended production format

-   **Canvas:** 9:16 vertical, 1080 × 1920.
-   **Duration:** 20--35 seconds for most concepts; 35--45 seconds for
    complex mechanisms.
-   **Frame rate:** 30 fps is a practical default; keep motion smooth
    and consistent.
-   **Style:** Premium cinematic 3D / motion graphics, deep navy or
    near-black environment, white labels, electric teal for
    fields/current/important motion, warm amber for energy, reaction
    progress, or answer reveal.
-   **Audio:** No narration by default. Restrained mechanical clicks,
    magnetic snaps, low impacts, soft electrical hums, and a short
    activation sound. Sound should support, not overwhelm, the learning.
-   **Typography:** Large, high-contrast, minimal. Prefer one main idea
    per screen. Keep formulas legible on a phone.
-   **Camera:** Slow push-ins, orbiting macro shots, controlled
    parallax, and one decisive reveal. Avoid constant camera movement.
-   **Text density:** One hook, 2--4 component labels, one principle,
    one formula/rule, one final takeaway.
-   **Branding:** Small PYQVault / InstaRead mark near the end; do not
    let branding compete with the concept.
-   **Reusable visual grammar:** exploded view → parts align →
    connections form → system activates → scientific principle is
    highlighted → exam takeaway.

## 1.3 Visual meaning system

Use these conventions consistently. They are a visual language, not
literal colours found in nature.

-   **White:** physical components, axes, labels, neutral structure.
-   **Electric teal:** current, field lines, wavefronts, electron/ion
    movement when appropriate, rays, or a highlighted process.
-   **Warm amber:** energy input/output, reaction progress, heat, key
    answer, or the "aha" moment.
-   **Muted red:** resistance, repulsion, danger, or a clearly defined
    negative/ opposing quantity---only where useful.
-   **Dotted/ghosted lines:** paths, trajectories, invisible geometry,
    or previous positions.
-   **Arrows:** direction of force, velocity, current, ion movement, or
    energy transfer. Always label what an arrow means.
-   **Particle trails:** use only when the path is a legitimate model of
    the phenomenon. Do not imply that field lines are physical strings
    or that electron motion is always a stream of visible balls.

## 1.4 Scientific integrity rules

1.  **Separate metaphor from mechanism.** Mechanical assembly is a
    visual metaphor; the real scientific process must still be
    represented accurately.
2.  **Show vectors explicitly.** For force, velocity, magnetic field,
    electric field, and current, label directions and use right-hand
    rules where relevant.
3.  **Do not over-literalize invisible phenomena.** Field lines are
    diagrams; electron orbitals are probability distributions; molecular
    motion is simplified.
4.  **Avoid misleading atom models.** Do not present electrons as tiny
    planets on fixed circular tracks except when explicitly identifying
    the Bohr model as a simplified model.
5.  **Use the right reaction story.** Show reactants, bonds broken,
    bonds formed, and products only when the chemistry supports it. Do
    not imply atoms are created or destroyed in ordinary chemical
    reactions.
6.  **Show energy correctly.** Exothermic reactions release energy
    overall; endothermic reactions absorb energy overall. Activation
    energy is not the same as total reaction enthalpy.
7.  **Use qualitative visuals before equations.** The viewer should
    understand the motion before the formula appears.
8.  **Keep diagrams legible.** If a 3D cinematic effect makes a diagram
    hard to read, switch to a clean 2D overlay.
9.  **Check all equations, units, signs, and directions** against a
    trusted syllabus/reference before publishing.
10. **Do not claim that an animation proves a concept.** It illustrates
    a model. The captions should state the applicable assumptions.

                                      component /       question
                                      finished object   
                                      silhouette

                                      enter             

  6--12 s           Assemble          Parts align,      Labels appear
                                      connect, or       only when needed
                                      interact

                                      reaction, or      label
                                      transfer begins

**For 20-second edits:** merge the reveal and assembly beats.\

state (e.g., motor vs generator, exothermic vs endothermic).

# 3. PHYSICS --- MECHANICS AND MOTION

## P01. Projectile motion: build the trajectory

**Hook:** "One launch. Two independent motions."\
**Learning objective:** Horizontal and vertical components combine to
create a parabolic trajectory (ignoring air resistance).

### Storyboard (25--30 s)

-   **0--2 s --- Hook:** A small metallic projectile floats above a dark
    launch platform. A reticle locks onto it. Text: **"Why is the path
    curved?"**
-   **2--5 s --- Build the launcher:** The platform assembles from three
    parts: base, launch arm, angle dial. The dial locks at an angle
    (`\theta`{=tex}).
-   **5--9 s --- Launch:** The projectile launches. Freeze briefly at
    the release point; split its velocity vector into (v_x) (teal,
    horizontal) and (v_y) (amber, vertical).
-   **9--15 s --- Show independence:** Horizontal ghost markers advance
    equal distances in equal time intervals. Vertical markers rise,
    slow, stop at the apex, then descend. The two motions combine into a
    curved path.
-   **15--21 s --- Reveal the model:** Overlay axes and a clean
    parabolic trace. Show (v_x=v_0`\cos`{=tex}`\theta`{=tex}),
    (v_y=v_0`\sin`{=tex}`\theta`{=tex}-gt).
-   **21--27 s --- Exam payoff:** Highlight: **"Horizontal acceleration
    = 0; vertical acceleration = −g."** End on the trajectory and a tiny
    PYQVault mark.

**Accuracy checks:** State "neglecting air resistance." Gravity acts
throughout the flight, including at the apex. At the apex, vertical
velocity is zero but acceleration is still (g) downward.\
**Production note:** A highly reusable visual; change launch angle or
initial speed to create follow-up videos.

## P02. Newton's laws: the force-and-motion assembly

**Hook:** "What actually changes an object's motion?"\
**Learning objective:** Net force causes acceleration; balanced forces
mean zero acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity.

### Storyboard (25--30 s)

-   **0--3 s:** A metal block floats in darkness. Text: **"Force ≠
    motion."**
-   **3--7 s:** Two force modules attach on opposite sides. Equal arrows
    appear; the block remains at rest or continues at constant velocity.
-   **7--12 s:** One module grows stronger. Opposing arrows become
    unequal; the net-force arrow appears.
-   **12--18 s:** The block accelerates. Position markers spread farther
    apart each frame.
-   **18--23 s:** A clean vector diagram assembles beside the block:
    (`\sum `{=tex}F=ma).
-   **23--29 s:** Two states side by side: balanced forces → (a=0);
    unbalanced force → (a`\neq0`{=tex}). Final text: **"Acceleration
    follows net force."**

**Accuracy checks:** Do not depict a continuing force as necessary to
maintain constant velocity in an ideal frictionless setting. Distinguish
force from velocity.\
**Production note:** Use the same rig for friction, tension, normal
reaction, and inclined-plane problems.

## P03. Conservation of momentum: collision lock-up

**Hook:** "What stays constant when objects collide?"\
**Learning objective:** Total momentum is conserved in an isolated
system.

### Storyboard

-   **0--2 s:** Two metal pucks face each other on a
    frictionless-looking track.
-   **2--6 s:** Each puck assembles with a mass label and a velocity
    arrow. One moves fast; the other is stationary.
-   **6--11 s:** They collide in slow motion. A bright contact pulse
    marks the interaction.
-   **11--17 s:** The pucks separate or move together, depending on the
    chosen collision. A momentum ledger shows
    (m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2).
-   **17--23 s:** Before and after momentum arrows are placed on a
    balance scale; total vector momentum matches.
-   **23--29 s:** Final takeaway: **"Momentum transfers between objects;
    total momentum stays constant in an isolated system."**

**Accuracy checks:** Use a one-dimensional collision for a first
version. Do not imply kinetic energy is always conserved; it is
conserved in an ideal elastic collision, not generally in an inelastic
one.\
**Production note:** Create a paired Reel: elastic vs inelastic
collision.

## P04. Work, energy and power: energy transfer machine

**Hook:** "Where does the energy go?"\
**Learning objective:** Work transfers energy; energy changes form;
power is the rate of energy transfer.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A compact machine is dark and motionless. Text:
    **"Activate the energy chain."**
-   **3--8 s:** Input module attaches (a raised mass, spring, or
    motor---choose one). An energy bar fills.
-   **8--14 s:** The system runs: potential energy converts to kinetic
    energy, or electrical energy to mechanical energy.
-   **14--20 s:** A transparent overlay labels each stage. Use flowing
    energy bands rather than unexplained glowing particles.
-   **20--25 s:** Formula reveal: (W=`\Delta `{=tex}K); (P=W/t) (for
    average power).
-   **25--30 s:** Final text: **"Energy changes form. Power tells you
    how fast."**

**Accuracy checks:** Choose a single clean system per video. Distinguish
work done by a force from energy stored as potential energy.\
**Production note:** A visual template for spring energy, gravitational
potential energy, and efficiency.

## P05. Simple harmonic motion: spring oscillator

**Hook:** "Why does it keep returning?"\
**Learning objective:** Restoring force points toward equilibrium and is
proportional to displacement for ideal SHM.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A spring and mass float apart as an exploded mechanical
    assembly.
-   **3--7 s:** The spring attaches to a fixed support; the mass clicks
    onto its end.
-   **7--12 s:** Pull the mass from equilibrium and release it. A
    glowing equilibrium line stays fixed.
-   **12--18 s:** Motion oscillates; a displacement-time trace draws
    itself in sync.
-   **18--23 s:** At maximum displacement, speed is zero; at
    equilibrium, speed is maximum. Use two frozen frames to compare.
-   **23--29 s:** Show (F=-kx), (T=2`\pi`{=tex}`\sqrt{m/k}`{=tex}) for
    an ideal mass--spring system. Final text: **"Restoring force points
    back to equilibrium."**

**Accuracy checks:** The period formula is for an ideal mass--spring
system; do not apply it to every oscillator. Make the negative sign
meaningful by showing force opposite displacement.\
**Production note:** Reuse for pendulum motion, with its own
assumptions.

## P06. Pendulum: the restoring-force reveal

**Hook:** "What controls a pendulum's period?"\
**Learning objective:** For small oscillations,
(T=2`\pi`{=tex}`\sqrt{L/g}`{=tex}), independent of bob mass.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A bob, string, and pivot float as separate pieces.
-   **3--7 s:** String connects to pivot; bob locks in. A length ruler
    unfolds.
-   **7--12 s:** Bob is displaced and released. A restoring-force arrow
    points toward equilibrium.
-   **12--18 s:** The bob swings through the center. A timer ticks; show
    equal small-angle cycles.
-   **18--24 s:** The length (L) expands, making the period visibly
    longer. Bob mass changes but the period display remains the same
    under the ideal model.
-   **24--30 s:** Formula: (T=2`\pi`{=tex}`\sqrt{L/g}`{=tex}). Final
    text: **"Small-angle period depends on length and gravity---not bob
    mass."**

**Accuracy checks:** State the small-angle approximation. Do not imply
mass independence under every real-world condition or at large
amplitudes.\
**Production note:** Excellent for a "change one variable" visual
series.

## P07. Circular motion: centripetal force

**Hook:** "What keeps an object turning?"\
**Learning objective:** Circular motion requires inward centripetal
acceleration and net force.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A metallic sphere and circular track appear.
-   **3--7 s:** Track assembles around the sphere; a tangent velocity
    arrow appears.
-   **7--12 s:** The sphere moves in a circle. An inward arrow labeled
    "net force" tracks its position.
-   **12--17 s:** Briefly remove the inward constraint; the sphere moves
    along the tangent, not outward.
-   **17--23 s:** Overlay (a_c=v\^2/r) and (F_c=mv\^2/r).
-   **23--29 s:** Final text: **"Centripetal means centre-seeking."**

**Accuracy checks:** Centripetal force is not an extra, separate force;
it is the net inward force supplied by tension, friction, gravity, or
another interaction. Avoid the misleading "centrifugal force throws it
outward" explanation in an inertial frame.\
**Production note:** Use a tether, orbital, and banked-turn variant.

## P08. Gravitation and satellite orbit

**Hook:** "Why doesn't a satellite simply fall down?"\
**Learning objective:** An orbiting satellite is continuously falling
around Earth under gravity.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Earth assembles from a glowing core, mantle, crust, and
    atmosphere layers (stylized, not to scale).
-   **3--7 s:** A satellite snaps into place at a chosen altitude; a
    velocity arrow appears tangent to the orbit.
-   **7--13 s:** Gravity vector points toward Earth. The satellite
    advances while its path curves around the planet.
-   **13--18 s:** Show a short tangent path, then the curved orbital
    path. Text: **"It falls---and keeps missing the ground."**
-   **18--24 s:** Overlay (F=GMm/r\^2) and
    (v\_`\text{orbit}`{=tex}=`\sqrt{GM/r}`{=tex}) for a circular orbit.
-   **24--30 s:** Show a higher circular orbit with a lower orbital
    speed. Final text: **"Gravity provides centripetal force."**

**Accuracy checks:** Use distance (r) from Earth's centre in formulas.
Distinguish altitude from orbital radius. Avoid suggesting gravity is
absent in orbit.\
**Production note:** Escape velocity can be a separate sequel:
(v_e=`\sqrt{2GM/r}`{=tex}), ignoring atmosphere and other bodies.

## P09. Escape velocity: leave the gravity well

**Hook:** "How fast must a launch begin to escape?"\
**Learning objective:** Escape speed is the minimum launch speed needed
to reach infinitely far away with zero residual speed in an idealized
gravitational field.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A planet sits at the bottom of a deep, stylized
    gravitational well.
-   **3--7 s:** A launch vehicle assembles in stages. A speed gauge
    appears.
-   **7--13 s:** At low speed, the vehicle rises then falls back. At a
    higher speed, it travels farther.
-   **13--19 s:** At escape speed, the vehicle continues outward while
    its speed gradually decreases toward zero at infinity in the ideal
    model.
-   **19--25 s:** Formula reveal: (v_e=`\sqrt{2GM/r}`{=tex}).
-   **25--31 s:** Final text: **"Escape speed depends on the planet and
    launch distance---not the spacecraft's mass."**

**Accuracy checks:** This is an idealization; ignore atmosphere,
propulsion after launch, and other celestial bodies. Escape does not
mean gravity becomes zero.\
**Production note:** Use a clear energy-bar visualization to show
kinetic energy converting to gravitational potential energy.

## P10. Buoyancy: why objects float

**Hook:** "Why does a heavy ship float?"\
**Learning objective:** Buoyant force equals the weight of displaced
fluid.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A metal block and a fluid tank assemble from transparent
    panels.
-   **3--7 s:** The block enters the fluid; the fluid level rises.
-   **7--12 s:** Pressure arrows on the block are shown longer at
    greater depth. The net upward force arrow appears.
-   **12--18 s:** A ship-shaped hollow structure assembles and displaces
    a larger volume of water; it floats.
-   **18--24 s:** A volume marker highlights displaced fluid. Formula:
    (F_B=`\rho`{=tex}*`\text{fluid}`{=tex}gV*`\text{displaced}`{=tex}).
-   **24--30 s:** Final text: **"Floating depends on average density and
    displaced fluid---not simply on whether the material is metal."**

**Accuracy checks:** Show pressure increasing with depth. Explain that a
floating object's buoyant force balances its weight. Avoid implying that
the upward pressure is larger everywhere; the net force comes from the
pressure difference.\
**Production note:** Great for a visually satisfying cutaway of a ship's
hollow hull.

## P11. Friction and inclined planes

**Hook:** "Why does a block stay still---or start sliding?"\
**Learning objective:** Along an incline, gravity has a component down
the slope; friction opposes relative motion or its tendency.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A ramp and block float separately.
-   **3--7 s:** Ramp locks at an angle; block lands on it. Angle
    indicator appears.
-   **7--12 s:** Gravity arrow splits into parallel and perpendicular
    components.
-   **12--17 s:** A friction arrow appears opposite the impending or
    actual motion. Increase the ramp angle until sliding begins.
-   **17--23 s:** Clean free-body diagram:
    (mg`\sin`{=tex}`\theta`{=tex}), (mg`\cos`{=tex}`\theta`{=tex}),
    normal force, friction.
-   **23--30 s:** Final text: **"Static friction adjusts up to a limit;
    kinetic friction acts during sliding."**

**Accuracy checks:** Do not show static friction as always equal to
(`\mu`{=tex}\_sN); that is its maximum magnitude. State the model and
surface assumptions.\
**Production note:** Ideal for teaching free-body diagrams as
"assembling the forces."

# 4. PHYSICS --- ELECTRICITY AND MAGNETISM

## P12. Electric field: assemble the invisible map

**Hook:** "You can't see the field---but you can map it."\
**Learning objective:** Electric field direction is the force direction
on a positive test charge.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A charged sphere floats in a dark space. Text: **"What
    surrounds a charge?"**
-   **3--7 s:** A second, small test charge appears; a force arrow
    indicates the interaction.
-   **7--13 s:** Field lines draw outward for a positive source charge;
    repeat inward for a negative charge in a split-screen comparison.
-   **13--18 s:** Place two charges; field-line pattern changes. A test
    charge follows the force direction at a point.
-   **18--24 s:** Formula: (`\vec `{=tex}E=`\vec `{=tex}F/q). Optionally
    show (E=k\|Q\|/r\^2) for a point charge's magnitude.
-   **24--30 s:** Final text: **"Field direction is defined using a
    positive test charge."**

**Accuracy checks:** Field lines do not cross; line density represents
field strength qualitatively. Do not portray them as material strings.\
**Production note:** Create variants for like/unlike charges and uniform
fields between plates.

## P13. Electric potential and voltage

**Hook:** "Why does charge move between two points?"\
**Learning objective:** Electric potential difference is energy change
per unit charge; electric field and potential are related.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Two conductive plates assemble with a gap between them.
-   **3--7 s:** A voltage source locks into the circuit; one plate is
    labelled higher potential, the other lower potential.
-   **7--13 s:** A positive test charge is moved between points. An
    energy meter shows potential energy change.
-   **13--19 s:** Equipotential surfaces appear as clean translucent
    sheets; field arrows cross them perpendicularly.
-   **19--25 s:** Formula: (`\Delta `{=tex}V=`\Delta `{=tex}U/q); for a
    uniform field, (\|`\Delta `{=tex}V\|=Ed) along the field direction's
    magnitude relation.
-   **25--30 s:** Final text: **"Voltage is energy per unit charge."**

**Accuracy checks:** Be precise about sign conventions. In a uniform
field, potential decreases in the direction of the electric field. Do
not conflate voltage with current.\
**Production note:** Can connect to circuits, capacitors, and
electrostatics.

## P14. Resistors in series and parallel

**Hook:** "Same components. Different circuit behaviour."\
**Learning objective:** Series and parallel arrangements produce
different equivalent resistances and current/voltage distributions.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Battery, wires, and three resistors assemble as separate
    modules.
-   **3--7 s:** First configuration snaps into a single loop. Current
    arrows show the same current through series elements.
-   **7--12 s:** Voltage markers split across resistors; total voltage
    equals the sum of drops.
-   **12--17 s:** The circuit rearranges into parallel branches. Current
    splits; each branch has the same voltage across it.
-   **17--23 s:** Equations appear: (R_s=R_1+R_2+`\cdots`{=tex});
    (1/R_p=1/R_1+1/R_2+`\cdots`{=tex}).
-   **23--30 s:** Final side-by-side: series equivalent resistance
    increases; adding a parallel branch decreases equivalent resistance.

**Accuracy checks:** Use an ideal battery and wires for the basic model.
Do not depict current as being "used up" by a resistor.\
**Production note:** Use the same animated components for Ohm's law and
power.

## P15. Capacitor: storing charge and energy

**Hook:** "How can two plates store energy?"\
**Learning objective:** A capacitor stores separated charge and energy
in an electric field.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Two conductive plates and an insulating spacer float in
    a dark scene.
-   **3--7 s:** Plates align with the dielectric between them; wires
    connect to a battery.
-   **7--13 s:** Positive and negative charges accumulate on opposite
    plates; field visualization appears in the gap.
-   **13--18 s:** Disconnect the battery; the stored field remains in
    the idealized capacitor.
-   **18--24 s:** Show (Q=CV) and (U=`\frac`{=tex}12CV\^2).
-   **24--30 s:** Final text: **"A capacitor stores energy in its
    electric field."**

**Accuracy checks:** Charges on opposite plates are equal in magnitude
for an ideal two-plate capacitor. The dielectric is an insulator, not a
wire connecting the plates.\
**Production note:** Use a clean cutaway, with charge symbols kept
sparse.

## P16. Magnetic force on a current-carrying wire

**Hook:** "Can a magnetic field push a wire?"\
**Learning objective:** A current-carrying conductor experiences a force
in a magnetic field.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Two pole pieces and a wire segment float apart.
-   **3--7 s:** The magnet poles assemble; field arrows run from north
    to south outside the magnet.
-   **7--12 s:** The wire snaps into the field; current direction is
    labelled.
-   **12--17 s:** A force arrow appears perpendicular to both current
    and field. The wire moves.
-   **17--23 s:** Reverse current; force reverses. Reverse field; force
    reverses again.
-   **23--30 s:** Formula: (F=BIL`\sin`{=tex}`\theta`{=tex}). Final
    text: **"Force depends on field, current, length in the field, and
    angle."**

**Accuracy checks:** Specify conventional current direction. At
(`\theta=0`{=tex}\^`\circ`{=tex}), the force is zero; at
(90\^`\circ`{=tex}), it is maximum.\
**Production note:** This is one of the strongest candidates for the
assembly aesthetic.

## P17. Electric motor: electricity to rotation

**Hook:** "How does a current become motion?"\
**Learning objective:** Magnetic forces on a current-carrying coil
create torque.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Rotor, stator magnets, coil, axle and commutator float
    in an exploded view.
-   **3--8 s:** Stator magnets attach; coil mounts on the rotor; axle
    and brushes connect.
-   **8--13 s:** Current enters the coil. Opposite sides experience
    forces in opposite directions, producing torque.
-   **13--18 s:** The coil rotates. The commutator reverses current at
    the appropriate point in a simple DC motor model so torque continues
    in the same rotational direction.
-   **18--24 s:** Top-down cutaway labels: field, current, force,
    torque.
-   **24--30 s:** Final text: **"Electrical energy → mechanical
    energy."**

**Accuracy checks:** Show the commutator only for a brushed DC motor. Do
not mix a simple DC motor diagram with an AC induction motor.\
**Production note:** A flagship Reel. Consider a 35-second version for
clarity.

## P18. Electromagnetic induction: generate electricity

**Hook:** "Can motion create voltage?"\
**Learning objective:** Changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces
an EMF.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A magnet and copper coil hover in an exploded view.
-   **3--7 s:** Coil windings assemble around a core; a meter and
    circuit connect.
-   **7--12 s:** Move the magnet toward the coil; the meter deflects.
-   **12--17 s:** Stop the magnet; the meter returns to zero. Pull the
    magnet away; the deflection reverses.
-   **17--23 s:** Overlay magnetic-flux change and Faraday's law:
    (`\mathcal `{=tex}E=-N,d`\Phi`{=tex}\_B/dt).
-   **23--30 s:** Final text: **"No change in flux → no induced EMF in
    this setup."**

**Accuracy checks:** The minus sign expresses Lenz's law: induced
effects oppose the change in flux. A stationary magnet and stationary
coil do not induce EMF merely because a field exists.\
**Production note:** Use arrow direction and meter polarity to make the
reversal unmistakable.

## P19. AC generator: mechanical to electrical energy

**Hook:** "How does rotation produce alternating current?"\
**Learning objective:** Rotating a coil in a magnetic field changes
magnetic flux and induces an alternating EMF.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Magnet poles, coil, shaft, slip rings and brushes
    assemble separately.
-   **3--8 s:** The coil mounts between poles and the shaft locks into a
    rotating drive.
-   **8--14 s:** Rotation begins; field lines cross the coil. A voltage
    trace starts drawing.
-   **14--20 s:** As the coil rotates through half a turn, the induced

-   **25--31 s:** Final text: **"Mechanical rotation → alternating
    electrical output."**

**Accuracy checks:** Use slip rings for a basic AC generator; a

angular speed in the idealized model.\
**Production note:** Make the waveform synchronized to the rotating
coil.

## P20. Transformer: changing voltage with induction

**Accuracy checks:** A transformer needs changing magnetic flux; an

**Production note:** Great candidate for a pair of "step-up / step-down"

## P21. Electromagnetic waves: electric and magnetic fields propagate

## P22. Refraction and total internal reflection

**Hook:** "Why does light bend---and sometimes stay trapped?"\

## P23. Lenses and image formation

## P24. Wave interference and standing waves

## P25. Doppler effect: motion changes observed frequency

## P28. Photoelectric effect: light ejects electrons

## P29. Atomic spectra: quantized energy levels

## P30. Nuclear fission and fusion

## P31. Semiconductor diode: one-way current

**Production note:** Use a microscopic cutaway plus a clear circuit

    energy levels remain the same, while the catalysed peak is lower.
-   **24--31 s:** Final text: **"A catalyst lowers activation energy; it
    does not change (`\Delta `{=tex}H) or the equilibrium constant."**

**Accuracy checks:** Do not show the catalyst as consumed overall. A
catalyst changes the rate of reaching equilibrium, not the equilibrium
position for a given system.\
**Production note:** Use a literal "alternate route" visual metaphor.

## C14. Chemical equilibrium: dynamic balance

**Hook:** "At equilibrium, reactions haven't stopped."\
**Learning objective:** At dynamic equilibrium in a closed system,
forward and reverse reaction rates are equal.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Reactant and product chambers connect through a
    reversible pathway.
-   **3--8 s:** Reactant particles convert to products; product
    particles begin converting back.
-   **8--14 s:** Both directions continue. Rate meters rise, then become
    equal.
-   **14--20 s:** Concentration bars settle at constant values while
    particles still move.
-   **20--26 s:** Display
    (r\_`\text{forward}`{=tex}=r\_`\text{reverse}`{=tex}). Optionally
    show (K) only for a verified reaction.
-   **26--31 s:** Final text: **"Dynamic equilibrium: equal rates,
    constant macroscopic concentrations."**

**Accuracy checks:** Equal rates do not mean equal concentrations. State
that equilibrium is for a closed system under fixed conditions.\
**Production note:** Animate particles in both directions even after the
concentration bars stabilize.

## C15. Le Chatelier's principle

**Hook:** "How does equilibrium respond to a disturbance?"\
**Learning objective:** A system at equilibrium shifts in a direction
that counteracts an imposed change, within the principle's limits.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A reversible reaction is shown with stable concentration
    bars.
-   **3--8 s:** Add a reactant. Its bar rises and the system shifts
    toward products.
-   **8--14 s:** In a separate cut, change temperature for a specified
    exothermic or endothermic reaction; the equilibrium composition
    shifts.
-   **14--20 s:** For a gas equilibrium, change pressure/volume and show
    the direction based on gas mole counts, if applicable.
-   **20--26 s:** Label each disturbance and response; never combine
    multiple disturbances in one unexplained event.
-   **26--32 s:** Final text: **"Predict the shift from the disturbance
    and the reaction conditions."**

**Accuracy checks:** Temperature effects depend on reaction enthalpy.
Pressure effects matter for gas equilibria with different total gaseous
stoichiometric amounts. A catalyst does not shift equilibrium.\
**Production note:** Use separate short Reels for concentration,
temperature and pressure rather than overstuffing one.

## C16. Reaction rate and collision theory

**Hook:** "Why do some reactions happen faster?"\
**Learning objective:** Reaction rate depends on effective collisions;
temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts can affect rate.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A chamber fills with moving reactant particles.
-   **3--8 s:** At low concentration, particles collide infrequently.
-   **8--13 s:** Increase concentration; collision frequency rises.
    Highlight only collisions with suitable orientation/energy as
    effective.
-   **13--18 s:** Increase temperature; particles move faster and a
    greater fraction exceed activation energy.
-   **18--24 s:** A reaction-progress counter rises at different rates
    in the comparison panels.
-   **24--31 s:** Final text: **"More collisions are not enough;
    collisions must be effective."**

**Accuracy checks:** This is a simplified particle model. Avoid implying
every collision produces products.\
**Production note:** Excellent for particle-based animation and clear
A/B comparisons.

## C17. Oxidation and reduction: electron transfer

**Hook:** "Who loses electrons---and who gains them?"\
**Learning objective:** Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is
gain of electrons. They occur together.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Two half-cell regions and a wire assemble.
-   **3--8 s:** An electron leaves the species being oxidized; its
    oxidation state changes.
-   **8--14 s:** The electron travels through the external wire to the
    species being reduced.
-   **14--20 s:** Oxidation and reduction labels lock onto the
    respective half-reactions.
-   **20--26 s:** Show "OIL RIG" as an optional memory cue: Oxidation Is
    Loss, Reduction Is Gain. Include oxidation-state changes for the
    selected example.
-   **26--31 s:** Final text: **"Oxidation and reduction always occur
    together."**

**Accuracy checks:** Electron flow and conventional current point in
opposite directions in the external circuit. Check half-reaction
balancing in acidic/basic medium where relevant.\
**Production note:** A clean, directional visual; avoid overcomplicating
it with too many ions.

## C18. Galvanic cell: chemical energy to electricity

**Hook:** "Can a spontaneous reaction power a circuit?"\
**Learning objective:** A galvanic cell uses a spontaneous redox
reaction to generate electrical energy.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Two half-cells, electrodes, salt bridge and external
    wire assemble.
-   **3--8 s:** Metal electrodes enter their solutions; the salt bridge
    connects the half-cells.
-   **8--14 s:** Oxidation occurs at the anode; electrons flow through
    the wire toward the cathode.
-   **14--20 s:** Ions move through the salt bridge to maintain charge
    balance; a voltmeter shows a potential difference.
-   **20--26 s:** Label anode/cathode and oxidation/reduction. Add a
    verified cell notation for the chosen example.
-   **26--32 s:** Final text: **"Galvanic cell: redox chemistry drives
    electron flow."**

**Accuracy checks:** For a galvanic cell, anode is negative and cathode
positive under the usual convention; oxidation is at the anode,
reduction at the cathode. Ionic movement through the salt bridge is not
electron flow.\
**Production note:** Use a verified Daniell cell or another
syllabus-appropriate example.

## C19. Electrolysis: electricity drives a non-spontaneous change

**Hook:** "Can electricity force a chemical reaction?"\
**Learning objective:** Electrolysis uses electrical energy to drive a
non-spontaneous redox process.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Electrolytic cell, electrolyte, two electrodes and DC
    supply assemble.
-   **3--8 s:** Power supply connects; polarity labels appear.
-   **8--14 s:** Cations move toward the cathode and anions toward the
    anode; electrode reactions are shown for a chosen electrolyte.
-   **14--20 s:** Products appear at the electrodes according to the
    selected chemistry; a current indicator stays active.
-   **20--26 s:** Show the balanced electrode half-reactions and the
    overall reaction for the chosen example.
-   **26--32 s:** Final text: **"Electrolysis converts electrical energy
    into chemical change."**

**Accuracy checks:** Products depend on electrolyte, electrode material,
concentration and conditions. Do not use a generic product reveal
without specifying the system. In an electrolytic cell, the cathode is
negative and the anode positive.\
**Production note:** Must be reaction-specific; validate the chemistry
before rendering.

## C20. pH and acid--base neutralization

**Hook:** "What changes when acid meets base?"\
**Learning objective:** Acids and bases change the concentration of
relevant species; neutralization can form water and a salt depending on
the reaction.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Two transparent chambers assemble, one labelled acid and
    one base.
-   **3--8 s:** Show a simplified ion-level view appropriate to the
    chosen strong acid/base example.
-   **8--14 s:** Streams combine; (H\^+) (more accurately (H_3O\^+) in
    aqueous solution) and (OH\^-) form water.
-   **14--20 s:** A pH indicator bar moves as solution composition
    changes.
-   **20--26 s:** Show (H_3O\^+ + OH\^- `\rightarrow `{=tex}2H_2O) and
    define pH as (-`\log`{=tex}\_{10}\[H\^+\]) in the usual simplified
    dilute-solution treatment.
-   **26--31 s:** Final text: **"pH is logarithmic: one unit represents
    a tenfold change in hydrogen-ion concentration."**

**Accuracy checks:** Use (H_3O\^+) for aqueous chemistry where
appropriate. The simple concentration definition has limitations in
non-ideal solutions; keep scope aligned with the syllabus.
Neutralization products depend on the acid/base system.\
**Production note:** Use a clean pH scale, not a dramatic "acid
dissolves everything" effect.

## C21. Solubility and precipitation

**Hook:** "When does a clear solution turn cloudy?"\
**Learning objective:** A precipitate forms when the ionic product
exceeds the relevant solubility condition.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Two clear solution chambers assemble.
-   **3--8 s:** Dissolved ions are represented as dispersed particles.
-   **8--14 s:** Solutions mix; ion pairs form a solid lattice when a
    specified insoluble product forms.
-   **14--20 s:** The precipitate grows and settles; spectator ions
    remain in solution.
-   **20--26 s:** Show the net ionic equation for a verified example,
    such as formation of AgCl(s) from suitable aqueous ions.
-   **26--31 s:** Final text: **"Precipitation removes selected ions
    from solution as a solid."**

**Accuracy checks:** Verify solubility rules and the exact reaction
conditions. Do not imply all ions combine into a solid; spectator ions
may remain dissolved.\
**Production note:** A visually satisfying lattice-assembly effect.

## C22. Organic functional groups: molecular snap-together

**Hook:** "One small group can change a molecule's behaviour."\
**Learning objective:** Functional groups help determine the

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A carbon skeleton assembles as a molecular framework.
-   **3--8 s:** A functional group snaps onto a chosen position: alcohol

-   **8--14 s:** The molecule's name and highlighted group appear.
-   **14--20 s:** Compare two molecules with the same or similar carbon

-   **20--26 s:** Show one verified characteristic property or reaction

-   **26--31 s:** Final text: **"Identify the functional group first; it
    narrows the chemistry to expect."**

**Accuracy checks:** The property or reaction must match the chosen
compound and conditions. Do not imply the functional group alone
determines every physical property.\
**Production note:** This is a series format, not one mega-video. Each
Reel should focus on one group or one comparison.

## C23. Isomerism: same formula, different structure

-   **26--31 s:** Final text: **"Same formula does not guarantee the

**Accuracy checks:** Use a valid isomer pair with the same molecular

**Production note:** A literal molecular assembly/reassembly sequence is

# 9. HIGH-VALUE CROSSOVER CONCEPTS

These concepts work especially well because the visual system can show
both a mechanism and a result.

## X01. Battery charging and discharging

**Hook:** "What changes inside a rechargeable battery?"\
**Learning objective:** In a rechargeable cell, electrochemical
reactions are driven in opposite directions during charging and
discharging, within the battery's operating limits.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A battery cutaway assembles: two electrodes, separator,
    electrolyte, external circuit.
-   **3--8 s:** **Discharge mode:** electrons flow through the external
    circuit; ions move through the electrolyte; the device powers on.
-   **8--14 s:** Battery state-of-charge indicator falls while electrode
    states change in a simplified representation.
-   **14--20 s:** Switch to **charge mode**: external power source
    connects; electron flow and ion movement reverse for the selected
    rechargeable chemistry.
-   **20--26 s:** Split-screen labels distinguish external electron flow
    from internal ionic transport.
-   **26--32 s:** Final text: **"Charging reverses the useful
    electrochemical process---within the cell's limits."**

**Accuracy checks:** Use a specified chemistry, such as lithium-ion, and
verify which species move in which direction. Avoid universal claims
that all batteries work identically.\
**Production note:** Strong modern-tech hook, but the microscopic
chemistry must be validated for the specific battery.

## X02. Solar cell: light to electricity

**Hook:** "How does sunlight create current?"\
**Learning objective:** Photons can generate electron--hole pairs in a
semiconductor; the junction's electric field helps separate charge
carriers.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A solar-cell panel assembles from semiconductor layers,
    contacts and protective glass.
-   **3--8 s:** Photons arrive; suitable photons generate electron--hole
    pairs.
-   **8--14 s:** The junction field separates carriers; electrons and
    holes move toward different contacts.
-   **14--20 s:** An external circuit connects; current flows and a
    small load lights up.
-   **20--26 s:** Label "photon absorption," "electron--hole pair,"
    "charge separation," and "external current."
-   **26--32 s:** Final text: **"A solar cell converts light energy into
    electrical energy."**

**Accuracy checks:** Not every photon is absorbed or produces a useful
carrier. Do not portray the junction field as creating energy; the
incident light supplies energy.\
**Production note:** A premium, highly shareable animation.

## X03. Fibre optics: trapping light

**Hook:** "How can light travel around a bend?"\
**Learning objective:** Optical fibres guide light through repeated
total internal reflection in a higher-index core surrounded by a
lower-index cladding.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Core, cladding and light source assemble in
    cross-section.
-   **3--8 s:** The core locks inside the cladding; refractive-index
    labels appear.
-   **8--14 s:** A ray enters within the acceptance conditions and
    reflects repeatedly at the core boundary.
-   **14--20 s:** The fibre bends gently; the ray remains guided in the
    schematic illustration.
-   **20--26 s:** Show
    (n\_`\text{core}`{=tex}\>n\_`\text{cladding}`{=tex}) and the
    total-internal-reflection condition.
-   **26--31 s:** Final text: **"A higher-index core and lower-index
    cladding guide light."**

**Accuracy checks:** Real fibres guide modes, not simply one perfect
ray; use ray optics as a simplified model. Excessive bends can cause
loss.\
**Production note:** Excellent visual: glowing light travelling through
a transparent cutaway.

## X04. Magnetic levitation: force balance

**Hook:** "Can magnetic forces hold something up?"\
**Learning objective:** Levitation requires a force balance and depends
on the magnetic system and its stability.

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** A platform and magnetized object assemble in a dark
    chamber.
-   **3--8 s:** Magnetic field visualization appears; force arrows show
    upward and downward contributions.
-   **8--14 s:** The object rises until the vertical forces balance.
-   **14--20 s:** A small displacement tests stability; show either
    restoring motion for a properly constrained/stabilized system or
    loss of stability for a simple unstable arrangement.
-   **20--26 s:** Label the relevant forces and system type.
-   **26--31 s:** Final text: **"Levitation needs force balance---and
    stable control or a suitable physical mechanism."**

**Accuracy checks:** Do not imply that any two ordinary permanent
magnets can produce stable free levitation in every orientation. Specify
the method: diamagnetism, superconductors, active control, or mechanical
constraints.\
**Production note:** Use only with a clear, scientifically accurate
example.

## X05. Nuclear energy and mass defect

**Hook:** "How can a tiny mass difference release huge energy?"\
**Learning objective:** Binding energy is related to mass defect through
(E=`\Delta `{=tex}mc\^2).

### Storyboard

-   **0--3 s:** Separate nucleons assemble into a nucleus.
-   **3--8 s:** A mass/energy ledger displays the total mass of free
    nucleons versus the bound nucleus.
-   **8--14 s:** The bound nucleus is shown at a lower total energy; the
    difference is represented as binding energy.
-   **14--20 s:** A reaction compares initial and final total rest mass;
    the mass difference maps to energy release or absorption.
-   **20--26 s:** Formula: (E=`\Delta `{=tex}mc\^2), with units and
    scale clearly labelled.
-   **26--31 s:** Final text: **"Mass difference corresponds to energy;
    it is not 'missing matter' in a conservation violation."**

**Accuracy checks:** Use a specific, verified nuclear example if
numerical values are shown. Keep total energy-momentum conservation in
mind.\
**Production note:** Use a ledger or balance visual rather than an
explosion.

# 12. Reusable animation components for Claude Code

The implementation should be data-driven. Build a library of animation
primitives once, then feed it concept-specific storyboard data.

## 12.1 Suggested primitive library

-   `ExplodedAssembly`: places components in an exploded view and
    animates them into their final positions.
-   `ComponentSnap`: controlled translation/rotation with a soft lock-in
    effect.
-   `OrbitCamera`: restrained camera movement around the object.
-   `CutawayReveal`: reveals an internal mechanism without changing the
    underlying geometry.
-   `VectorArrow`: labelled vector with direction, magnitude and colour.
-   `FieldLineSet`: deterministic field-line drawing for a specified
    idealized geometry.
-   `ParticleSystem`: controlled particle count, speed and paths.
-   `WavefrontAnimator`: phase-controlled wavefronts or sinusoidal
    fields.
-   `RayDiagram`: rays, normals, focal points, axes and intersection
    markers.
-   `CircuitBuilder`: batteries, resistors, coils, switches, meters and
    wires.
-   `MoleculeBuilder`: atom/bond models with correct connectivity and
    labels.
-   `EnergyDiagram`: reactant/product energy levels, activation barrier
    and reaction coordinate.
-   `GraphDraw`: draws axes, curves, labels and highlighted regions.
-   `FormulaReveal`: typesets a formula and highlights the relevant
    terms.
-   `CalloutLabel`: anchored label with collision-aware placement.
-   `SplitScreenCompare`: side-by-side states with synchronized timing.
-   `AnswerReveal`: final takeaway, formula or misconception correction.
-   `BrandOutro`: restrained PYQVault / InstaRead signature.

## 12.3 Rendering architecture

A practical route:

1.  **Concept specification:** A human-reviewed JSON/TypeScript object
    containing the learning objective, script, equation, assumptions and
    storyboard.
2.  **Scene selection:** Map each scene to deterministic primitives.
3.  **Asset generation:** Generate only assets that need unique geometry
    or texture. Use vector/3D procedural assets for diagrams, arrows,
    graphs and formulas.
4.  **Timeline assembly:** Drive every scene from the same composition
    clock; avoid independent timers that drift.
5.  **Validation:** Check equation text, component labels, scientific
    directions, safe margins, and frame-to-frame consistency.
6.  **Render:** Produce a vertical MP4 and a thumbnail.
7.  **Quality gate:** Review the output on a phone-sized preview before
    publishing.

**Important:** Do not make image-generation models responsible for
rendering formulas, labels, equations or precise diagrams. Render these
as real text/vector elements over the scene.

# 13. Quality-control checklist

Before a Reel is approved, verify:

## Science

-   [ ] The concept is stated in one sentence.
-   [ ] Every arrow has the correct direction and meaning.
-   [ ] The equation matches the visual and uses correct notation.
-   [ ] Units, signs, symbols, and assumptions are correct.
-   [ ] Simplifications are appropriate for the intended exam level.
-   [ ] The animation does not create a common misconception.
-   [ ] A subject-matter reviewer has checked non-trivial
    chemistry/reaction mechanisms.

## Visuals

-   [ ] The first two seconds contain a strong visual question.
-   [ ] The build-up reveals meaningful components, not random
    decoration.
-   [ ] The activation moment is synchronized to the explanation.
-   [ ] Labels remain legible at phone size.
-   [ ] Camera motion does not obscure the concept.
-   [ ] The end frame gives a clear learning payoff.
-   [ ] No copyrighted franchise design, logo, or footage is copied.

## Publishing

-   [ ] 9:16 vertical, 1080 × 1920.
-   [ ] Text stays inside platform-safe margins.
-   [ ] Captions are proofread.
-   [ ] Formula is readable for long enough to process.
-   [ ] Cover has a short, curiosity-driven headline.
-   [ ] Reel description includes the concept and a useful exam-relevant
    takeaway.
-   [ ] Track completion rate, average watch time, saves, shares, and
    follows.

# 14. Final strategic recommendation

Do not try to turn every syllabus line into a cinematic 3D build-up. Use
this format where the **assembly, motion, interaction or transformation
is itself explanatory**.

Use the format especially for: - mechanisms (motor, generator,
transformer, galvanic cell); - invisible forces and fields (electric
field, magnetic force, gravity); - state changes and transformations
(ionic bonding, molecular shape, reaction pathways); - wave behaviour
(refraction, interference, electromagnetic waves); - energy flow
(battery, solar cell, heat engine, photoelectric effect).

Use cleaner 2D motion graphics for concepts where precision and
comparison matter more than cinematic assembly---for example, long
derivations, detailed graphs, dense periodic-trend exceptions, and
multi-step numerical solutions.

The differentiator is not simply "beautiful science animation." It is
**beautiful science animation that leaves the student with one correct,
retrievable idea**.

Start with a five-video pilot, establish a recognizable visual language,
and turn the best-performing primitives into a repeatable production
pipeline for PYQVault InstaRead.
